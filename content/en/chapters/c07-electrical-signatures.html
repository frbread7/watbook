Use electrical patterns to rank falsifiable hypotheses without claiming root cause.

Read a signature as a constrained observation

An electrical signature is a pattern among related measured responses: for example, a shift in leakage with unchanged breakdown, a change in two geometry variants with different magnitude, or a site pattern that aligns with wafer radius. It can prioritize hypotheses, but it does not uniquely identify a process cause. Multiple mechanisms can create similar signatures; one mechanism can produce different responses across structures or test conditions.

Begin with quality and comparability checks: measurement contract, structure identity, test and extraction revisions, temperature, compliance, site coverage, coordinate transform, sampling, and matching baseline. Then state the observation with units, uncertainty, and the population. Do not name a mechanism before checking whether the observation is reproducible and not explained by measurement context.

Build a discriminating hypothesis table

For each candidate explanation, list the expected direction and pattern across independent structures, process records, and conditions; list evidence that would contradict it; and name the lowest-cost follow-up measurement that separates it from the alternatives. Prefer candidates with a clear falsifier over broad labels such as “process issue.” A useful hypothesis makes a prediction that could be wrong.

Ordinal evidence is not a probability

The diagnosis exercise assigns each selected observation a transparent −1, 0, or +1 support value for each fictional hypothesis. The total is an ordinal discussion aid only. It has no calibrated likelihood, prior, uncertainty interval, or causal interpretation. Equal totals are a useful signal that current evidence cannot distinguish the candidates.

A more formal model may be appropriate when the measurement design, data-generating process, and decision loss are specified. But machine-learning feature importance or a high predictive score does not by itself establish mechanism. Data leakage across wafers from the same lot, selection bias, unmodeled tool changes, or shifting test definitions can produce convincing but nonportable results.

Escalation requires decision context

Define when an engineer should hold, monitor, sample further, or release to the next step according to actual control plans and customer requirements. This textbook provides no manufacturing disposition limits. If a WAT result crosses an applicable internal limit, use the approved containment workflow; do not use a toy chart or rank score to override it. Link statistical-control methods to the independently authored YieldBook and future FabControlBook rather than treating WAT interpretation as a replacement for control design.

Knowledge check

A charge-related monitor and a leakage monitor both shift. Does this prove a dielectric process excursion? No. It motivates a hypothesis. Check the measurement conditions, compare orthogonal structures and process history, and plan evidence that distinguishes dielectric change from temperature, contamination, contact, or test-program effects.
