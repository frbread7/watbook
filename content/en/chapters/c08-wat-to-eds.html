Align related populations while guarding against lot confounding and data leakage.

Link related evidence without merging populations

WAT data are typically collected from dedicated monitor structures or selected sites; electrical die sort (EDS) tests product dies using a product test program. Their units, geometry, sample selection, and measurement conditions differ. In-line metrology measures process/material attributes rather than the same electrical response. A useful analysis aligns data by lot, wafer, process route, time, coordinate transform, and revision while retaining which population each source represents.

There are several possible questions: does WAT co-vary with EDS at the lot level; does WAT add prediction for a future lot beyond the current baseline; do monitor structures identify a failure mode not captured by product testing; or does a proposed process change shift both? These questions need different designs. Correlation among pooled die rows is not the same as validation on future lots.

Aggregation can reverse a relationship

The lab creates a deterministic fictional dataset with four lots. Within each lot, WAT rises as EDS falls; lot means, however, rise together. The exact construction is:

lotShift = lotIndex − 1.5; within = siteIndex − 4.5
WAT = 100 + 12·lotShift + within
EDS = 500 + 30·lotShift − 2·within

The pooled slope is positive while the within-lot slope is negative. This is a designed aggregation example, not a realistic manufacturing relationship. It shows why grouping and the intended prediction unit must be specified before fitting or interpreting coefficients.

For a prediction claim, use a validation split that respects the future-use unit. If the model will predict new lots, hold out whole lots. Randomly dividing rows from the same lots between training and test leaks shared lot context. Freeze feature selection and preprocessing inside training folds, define prediction horizon, compare to a simple baseline, and report uncertainty and failure cases.

Prediction limits and transportability

A model’s useful range is bounded by its training population, process window, test-program revisions, product mix, and measurement stability. A predictor may fail when the process changes, when a monitor is redesigned, or when an unrepresented product layout is introduced. Report calibration, sample counts at every hierarchy level, missingness, out-of-range input behavior, grouped validation, and the decision for which the output is intended.

Feature-selection papers and predictive studies can suggest questions and methods, but their coefficients, selected parameters, or error estimates are not transferable factory limits. This book’s synthetic lab does not train a predictive model, reproduce public research data, or establish a WAT-to-EDS mapping. No foundry relationship or proprietary acceptance rule is asserted.

Association is not causal attribution

Common cause, selection, temporal order, and measurement changes can create association. A process split with suitable controls, an independently measured mediator, repeated evidence across populations, and a physically justified model can strengthen a causal argument. Even then, distinguish evidence from assumptions and preserve alternative explanations. A lot-level correlation can help prioritize an experiment; it is not sufficient by itself to claim a process module caused a product shift.

Knowledge check

A pooled WAT/EDS correlation is strong, but it vanishes when entire lots are held out. Is the predictor production-ready? No. The intended future-lot use has not been validated, and pooled association may mainly encode lot identity.
