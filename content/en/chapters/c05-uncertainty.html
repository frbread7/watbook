Build an uncertainty argument and distinguish measurement variation from unit variation.

Uncertainty is part of the result

Measurement uncertainty describes the dispersion of values that could reasonably be attributed to the measurand given the measurement model and evidence. It is not synonymous with an instrument specification, repeatability, an engineering guard band, or a probability that the result is wrong. A result without an uncertainty statement can still be useful for ranking or monitoring, but the limits of comparison should be stated.

Start by defining the measurand and measurement model. Identify relevant sources such as calibration, resolution, repeatability, reproducibility, environmental influence, contact variation, setup, correction models, and extraction uncertainty. Decide which components are estimated from observed repeated data (Type A) and which use other information (Type B). Avoid counting one physical source twice in different categories.

First-order propagation and its limits

For an output y=f(x1,…,xn) with small input uncertainties and a locally linear response, first-order propagation gives the combined variance
uc²(y) ≈ Σi Σj (∂f/∂xi)(∂f/∂xj) cov(xi,xj). The combined standard uncertainty uc(y) is the square root of this propagated variance. If inputs are independent, this reduces to root-sum-square propagation. Correlations require covariance terms. Nonlinearity, asymmetric distributions, censoring, and model uncertainty may require a different treatment or Monte Carlo propagation. The GUM’s 2026 amendment addresses nonlinearity in measurement models; use current metrology guidance for formal uncertainty work.

The lab’s RSS budget takes declared, independent standard-uncertainty components in one common unit and computes uc=√Σui² plus U=k·uc. Its default k=2 is a teaching convention, not a guarantee of 95% coverage. Coverage depends on distribution, degrees of freedom, model, and reporting purpose.

Characterize the measurement process

Separate variation among physical units from measurement-system variation. Repeated readings of one stable structure under controlled conditions estimate short-term repeatability but do not capture wafer-to-wafer manufacturing spread. Reproducibility asks what changes across operators, tools, channels, days, or setups. Bias asks whether the method’s expectation differs from a suitable reference. Stability asks whether the measurement process changes over time.

A crossed or nested gauge study can help allocate these sources when its design matches the measurement workflow. The hierarchy matters: a destructive or drifting structure cannot be treated as stable merely because it was measured repeatedly. Reference standards, check sites, calibration records, and randomized trial order provide complementary evidence. NIST’s measurement-process handbook gives design and analysis guidance; actual engineering studies need a declared design and appropriate model.

Decision margin and guard band

Suppose an engineering limit is L and the measured value is y. A guard band changes the acceptance rule to account for the consequences of false accept and false reject decisions and for uncertainty. It is a decision policy, not a property of the instrument. The risk allocation, population, uncertainty model, customer requirement, and applicable procedures must be explicit. Do not infer product acceptance rules from this book’s teaching examples.

Before comparing two means, consider uncertainty in both, covariance from shared references or setup, and the sampling uncertainty of the populations. A statistically detectable shift may be too small to matter to a product; a practically important shift may be hidden by poor precision. Statistical significance and engineering significance answer different questions.

Knowledge check

Two independent standard uncertainty components are 3 mV and 4 mV. The lab reports 5 mV combined standard uncertainty and 10 mV expanded uncertainty for k=2. Does that prove 95% coverage? No. The arithmetic is correct under independence, but the coverage interpretation needs distribution and degrees-of-freedom assumptions.
