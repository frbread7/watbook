Define the question, evidence stream, sample hierarchy, and limits of a wafer-level claim.

Questions this chapter answers

  • Which decision is wafer acceptance test (WAT) intended to inform?
  • What does a parameter monitor actually observe?
  • Which conclusions require evidence beyond the wafer measurement?

WAT is a family of electrical measurements on designated monitor structures, commonly placed in scribe lanes or dedicated test sites. A process-control-monitor (PCM) program selects structures and conditions to observe particular electrical responses. It is not a universal measurement recipe: product, technology, customer, and factory definitions differ. Here, WAT means a documented set of wafer-level electrical monitor measurements used to understand process and device behavior before or alongside product-level test.

A chain of claims, not a single number

A WAT datum becomes useful only when its context survives the path from structure to decision. The minimum chain is: engineering question → monitor structure → measurand → physical stimulus and conditions → extraction rule → sampled population and coordinate frame → uncertainty and validity checks → interpretation → action or next measurement. Breaking a link changes what can responsibly be inferred.

For example, a median threshold-voltage estimate can summarize a population under one extraction convention. By itself it does not identify a particular implant excursion, prove a circuit timing failure, or establish a product-screen limit. A measured shift may reflect a changed device, a changed temperature, a different extraction method, a contact issue, or a new combination of these effects.

Decision statement template: “For [population], parameter [definition] measured under [conditions] changed by [estimate and uncertainty]. This supports [bounded interpretation]; it does not establish [alternative claim]. The next discriminating evidence is [measurement or split].”

Separate neighboring evidence streams

In-line metrology measures material or process attributes during fabrication, such as film thickness or critical dimension. WAT observes electrical responses on monitor structures. Electrical die sort (EDS), also called wafer sort in many flows, measures product dies against product test programs. Final test commonly follows packaging and uses a different physical and electrical context. These streams can be correlated and jointly informative, but they do not measure the same population or measurand.

A monitor structure can be more directly sensitive to a process step than a finished product circuit, but it is not necessarily representative of every product layout. A product may include stress, loading, layout, thermal, and circuit interactions that the monitor does not reproduce. Conversely, a monitor can expose a process shift before product test has enough samples to resolve it.

Use the data hierarchy explicitly

Wafer sites are nested within wafers; wafers are nested within lots; lots can be nested within process windows, tools, chambers, or time periods. A site count is not automatically an independent sample count. When the decision concerns lot-to-lot change, treating hundreds of sites from one lot as hundreds of independent lots inflates evidence. Preserve the identifiers needed to model the hierarchy and state the population to which the conclusion applies.

Every exported row should preserve at least a stable lot and wafer identifier, structure and parameter definition, test-program and limit revision, measurement timestamp, site coordinates and coordinate convention, instrument/prober/channel context, and applicable process-route context. Data governance rules may restrict which identifiers leave a factory; this book assumes all case data are synthetic.

Knowledge check

A monitor’s median threshold estimate shifts by 18 mV while its temperature metadata is missing. Can an engineer attribute the change to an implant step? No. First establish that the measurand, structure, extraction, temperature, and comparison population are compatible. Then use process history and independent measurements to test candidate mechanisms.
